\title{Fleet-Informed Bayesian Optimization for Data-Efficient Controller Calibration}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Jakob Weber, Markus Gurtner, Adrian Trachte, Andreas Kugi}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Draft author list -- affiliations to be finalized}}}

\begin{abstract}
Controller calibration is commonly repeated for every new system variant, although mature fleets may already contain substantial information about which controller-design parameters perform well. This paper studies whether historical fleet calibration experience can reduce the number of new closed-loop experiments required for a newly arriving client. We consider LQI yaw-rate tracking for a heterogeneous vehicle fleet and use Bayesian optimization (BO) to tune a three-dimensional controller-weight vector. Historical scalar calibration observations from previously optimized clients are used to warm-start the target client's Gaussian-process surrogate, while every candidate controller is synthesized from the target client's own identified model and evaluated only on its local nonlinear plant. A pre-registered confirmatory campaign with 20 previously untouched fleet seeds and 120 held-out targets shows that mature-fleet warm start reduces the mean number of target-local experiments required to reach within 5\% of the client-specific oracle from 6.63 to 3.07, a 54\% reduction, while strongly reducing variability. A dynamics-aware weighting of historical observations provides no measurable improvement over unweighted pooling. A separate landscape study explains this result: despite substantial plant heterogeneity, pairwise LQI-calibration landscapes remain highly rank-correlated, with Spearman correlation above 0.95 for every evaluated client pair. The results indicate that fleet-level controller-calibration knowledge can be broadly reusable even when the underlying plant dynamics differ substantially.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Controller calibration is an important but expensive part of commissioning modern dynamical systems. Even when the controller structure is fixed by model-based design, parameters such as LQR/LQI state and input penalties still determine the final compromise between tracking performance, control effort, and robustness. In practice, these parameters are often refined by repeated simulation or closed-loop testing. Bayesian optimization (BO) is attractive for this task because it can optimize low-dimensional controller parameters from a limited number of expensive zeroth-order evaluations~\cite{frazier2018tutorial,marco2016lqr,berkenkamp2016safe,schillinger2017safe,vonrohr2024local}.

A fleet setting changes the calibration problem fundamentally. Vehicles or machines are often commissioned one after another, and earlier clients have already accumulated closed-loop calibration experience. Repeating a cold-start optimization for every new client ignores this information. Transfer learning for BO addresses an analogous cold-start problem by reusing observations or surrogate information from related source tasks~\cite{joy2019transfer,tighineanu2022transfer,perrone2018scalable}. Collaborative and federated BO similarly exploit information distributed across multiple agents~\cite{dai2020fts,dai2021dpfts,chen2025collaborative}. For controller calibration, however, a central question remains unresolved: \emph{how transferable are previous calibration observations when the underlying dynamical systems are genuinely different?}

The intuitive answer is not obvious. Heterogeneous vehicle dynamics can change closed-loop behavior substantially, and our previous work showed that uncertain dynamics estimates contain useful structure for clustered system identification and controller design~\cite{weber2026ifac}. This motivates dynamics-aware transfer: historical observations from dynamically similar clients could be trusted more than observations from distant clients. At the same time, the controller-calibration problem may be much more invariant than the plant itself. Different models can induce similar orderings of the same low-dimensional LQI weight configurations, in which case broad reuse of fleet experience may be sufficient and explicit peer selection may add little.

This paper investigates that distinction in a mature-fleet setting. A new target client joins a fleet whose previous clients have already completed BO-based controller calibration. The historical clients provide scalar calibration records in the common controller-weight space. The target begins with only two local initialization experiments, fits a GP surrogate jointly to its own and the historical observations, and sequentially evaluates new target-specific LQI controllers. Historical observations do not count toward the target's experimental budget, and no fixed controller gain is transferred: every candidate gain is synthesized from the target client's own identified model.

The contribution is deliberately empirical and control-oriented rather than a new general BO transfer model. We make three main contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate \emph{mature-fleet controller calibration} as a transfer problem in which historical fleet calibration observations are reused to reduce the number of new closed-loop experiments required by a newly arriving client.
    \item We demonstrate, in a pre-registered held-out campaign on a heterogeneous nonlinear vehicle fleet, that fleet-informed warm start reduces the mean number of local evaluations needed to reach within 5\% of the client-specific oracle from 6.63 to 3.07 and strongly reduces the difficult-client tail.
    \item We characterize why this broad transfer is possible. Although the identified vehicle dynamics differ substantially, the LQI-calibration landscapes remain highly rank-correlated across all evaluated client pairs. Consistent with this observation, dynamics-aware weighting does not outperform simple unweighted pooling on fresh confirmatory data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Heterogeneous clients and local controller design}

Consider a fleet of clients indexed by $i\in\mathcal C$. The true plant of client $i$ is
\begin{align}
    x_{i,k+1} &= f_i(x_{i,k},u_{i,k},w_{i,k}),\\
    y_{i,k} &= h_i(x_{i,k},v_{i,k}),
\end{align}
where the maps $f_i$ differ across clients. Each client identifies a compact linear control-design model
\begin{equation}
    x_{k+1}=A(\theta_i)x_k+B(\theta_i)u_k,
    \label{eq:identified_model}
\end{equation}
with estimate $\hat\theta_i$. In the vehicle study, the state contains vehicle sideslip and yaw rate, $x=[\beta,r]^\top$. The true evaluation plant includes nonlinear tire saturation, whereas identification and LQI synthesis retain the lower-order linear model. This deliberate mismatch ensures that BO calibrates against closed-loop plant performance rather than merely optimizing the same model used for synthesis.

For yaw-rate tracking, the state is augmented with integral tracking error
\begin{equation}
    z_{k+1}=z_k+T_s(r_k^{\rm ref}-r_k).
\end{equation}
Let $x_k^a=[x_k^\top,z_k]^\top$ denote the augmented state. The LQI penalty is parameterized by a three-dimensional design vector $\xi\in\Xi\subset\mathbb R^3$,
\begin{align}
    Q(\xi)&=\operatorname{diag}(10^{\xi_1},10^{\xi_2},10^{\xi_3}),\\
    R&=1,
    \label{eq:lqi_weights}
\end{align}
with fixed physical state scaling applied before solving the Riccati equation. For client $i$, the target-specific feedback gain is
\begin{equation}
    K_i(\xi)=\operatorname{LQI}(\hat A_i,\hat B_i,\hat C_i,Q(\xi),R).
    \label{eq:lqi_gain}
\end{equation}
Thus, all clients share the same calibration coordinates $\xi$, but the resulting gain is client specific.

For calibration episode $\omega$, applying $K_i(\xi)$ to the true plant yields yaw-rate tracking loss $J_i(\xi;\omega)$. The client objective is the expected closed-loop loss
\begin{equation}
    f_i(\xi)=\mathbb E_{\omega}[J_i(\xi;\omega)],
    \label{eq:client_objective}
\end{equation}
subject to feasibility constraints on closed-loop stability, steering demand, and sideslip. The benchmark uses the same balanced multi-maneuver episode distribution for every client so that differences in $f_i$ are caused by plant heterogeneity rather than by different assigned tasks.

\subsection{Mature-fleet transfer objective}

Suppose a mature fleet contains historical calibration records
\begin{equation}
    \calD_j^{\rm hist}=\left\{(\xi_{j,m},\tilde y_{j,m})\right\}_{m=1}^{M_j},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde y_{j,m}$ is the source client's normalized calibration loss. A new target $i$ joins the fleet. Its own historical record is excluded from the source pool. The target performs $n_{\rm init}$ local initialization experiments and then sequentially selects new $\xi$ values by BO.

The quantity of practical interest is not the total amount of fleet data but the number of \emph{new target-local experiments}. Let $J_i^\star$ denote the target's offline oracle reference used only for evaluation. Define the first local evaluation count at which the incumbent reaches within 5\% of that oracle as
\begin{equation}
    N_{i,5\%}
    =\min\left\{n:\;J_i^{\rm best}(n)\le 1.05 J_i^\star\right\}.
    \label{eq:n5}
\end{equation}
The main research question is whether access to $\{\calD_j^{\rm hist}\}_{j\neq i}$ reduces $N_{i,5\%}$ relative to independent BO.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Fleet-Informed Bayesian Optimization}
\label{sec:method}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Normalized historical observations}

Historical objectives can have different physical scales across clients. We therefore normalize every source observation by a fixed client-local baseline controller,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde y_{j,m}=\frac{J_j(\xi_{j,m})}{J_j^{\rm base}}.
    \label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
The target applies the same normalization to its new local observations. The baseline is available before BO and is independent of the oracle used for reporting. Infeasible controller evaluations are assigned a fixed normalized penalty. This produces a common response scale on which historical records from different clients can be combined.

\subsection{Warm-started Gaussian-process surrogate}

The target fits a Gaussian-process surrogate to the union of its local data and the mature-fleet records. Let
\begin{equation}
    \calD_i^{(n)}=\calD_i^{\rm local}(n)\cup\bigcup_{j\neq i}\calD_j^{\rm hist}.
\end{equation}
The next candidate is chosen by expected improvement (EI),
\begin{equation}
    \xi_{i,n+1}\in\arg\max_{\xi\in\Xi}\operatorname{EI}_i(\xi\mid\calD_i^{(n)}).
    \label{eq:ei}
\end{equation}
The proposed $\xi_{i,n+1}$ is converted to a client-specific gain through~\eqref{eq:lqi_gain} and evaluated only on the target plant. The mature records are static prior information: no additional source-client experiment is required during target commissioning.

We evaluate three methods:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Independent BO}: only the target's own observations are used.
    \item \textbf{Global fleet warm start}: all mature-fleet records receive equal weight.
    \item \textbf{Dynamics-weighted warm start}: mature observations are weighted according to identified-dynamics similarity; this method is retained as a mechanistic ablation rather than the final proposed method.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dynamics-aware ablation}

To test whether source relevance needs to be modeled explicitly, we construct an uncertainty-aware dynamics distance
\begin{equation}
    (\distdyn_{ij})^2=(\hat\theta_i-\hat\theta_j)^\top
    (\hat\Sigma_i+\hat\Sigma_j+\epsilon I)^{-1}
    (\hat\theta_i-\hat\theta_j),
    \label{eq:dyn_distance}
\end{equation}
and similarity
\begin{equation}
    s_{ij}=\exp\left(-\frac{(\distdyn_{ij})^2}{2\ell_d^2}\right),
    \label{eq:similarity}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_d$ is the median pairwise-distance heuristic. The GP implementation converts lower similarity into larger observation noise,
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{ij}=\frac{\alpha_0}{\max(s_{ij},\epsilon_s)}.
    \label{eq:weighted_noise}
\end{equation}
This mechanism materially changes the surrogate and, for a nontrivial subset of targets, changes the first BO proposal. Its value is therefore tested empirically rather than assumed.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiments}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Vehicle fleet and task}

The benchmark contains three generating families with ten clients each: nominal, payload, and tire-degraded. All clients operate at the same nominal longitudinal speed. Relative to the nominal prototype, payload clients increase vehicle mass and yaw inertia, while tire-degraded clients reduce front tire cornering stiffness. The true plant uses nonlinear tire forces of the form
\begin{equation}
    F_y=\mu F_z\tanh\left(\frac{C_\alpha\alpha}{\mu F_z}\right),
    \label{eq:tire_nonlinearity}
\end{equation}
whereas the identified controller-design model remains linear. Every client is evaluated on the same two-maneuver lane-change distribution, with three calibration episodes per maneuver. A sampled client is admitted only when the fixed baseline controller is feasible on the standardized multi-maneuver bank.

The controller search space is three dimensional. Physical constraints include a sideslip bound of $6^\circ$ and a steering-rate bound of $0.2618$ rad per simulation step; synthesis or closed-loop failures are treated as infeasible. The primary performance output is yaw-rate RMSE.

\subsection{Landscape-similarity study}

To investigate whether dynamics heterogeneity translates into calibration heterogeneity, every selected pair of clients is evaluated on the same common Sobol design $\{\xi^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}$ in controller-weight space. For clients $i$ and $j$, calibration-landscape similarity is measured by
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{ij}^{J}=\operatorname{Spearman}\left(
        \{J_i(\xi^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^{M},
        \{J_j(\xi^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^{M}
    \right).
    \label{eq:landscape_similarity}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Pre-registered confirmatory campaign}

After the method and metrics were frozen on development data, the final campaign was run on 20 entirely new fleet seeds that had not been used for algorithm development or diagnostics. For every seed, all 30 mature clients first generate independent BO histories of 25 evaluations. Six target problems are then held out per seed, two from each family, giving 120 confirmatory targets. For every target, its own mature history is excluded and the remaining 29-client history pool is available at time zero.

Each target starts with $n_{\rm init}=2$ local Sobol evaluations and has a maximum budget of 12 target-local experiments. Independent, global-warm-start, and dynamics-weighted-warm-start BO use identical target initialization, candidate-evaluation code, and random seeds. The primary metric is $\Nfive$ from~\eqref{eq:n5}. Secondary metrics are the area under the oracle-relative regret curve over the early target budget and fixed-budget success probabilities.

Oracle-relative regret is
\begin{equation}
    r_i^\star(n)=\frac{J_i^{\rm best}(n)-J_i^\star}{J_i^\star}.
    \label{eq:regretstar}
\end{equation}
For inference, the fleet seed is the replication unit. Per seed, metrics are averaged over the six held-out targets, then compared across the 20 seeds using paired bootstrap confidence intervals and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

\subsection{Fleet warm start halves the required local experiments}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} shows the confirmatory oracle-relative regret as a function of new target-local experiments. Both fleet-informed methods improve markedly faster than independent BO. The separation occurs early and persists over the commissioning horizon, whereas global and dynamics-weighted warm start remain nearly indistinguishable.

The primary result is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}. Independent BO requires on average 6.63 local experiments to reach within 5\% of the client-specific oracle, compared with 3.07 for global fleet warm start. This corresponds to a 54\% reduction in mean local experimentation. The standard deviation decreases from 3.76 to 0.81, showing that warm start primarily removes the long tail of difficult cold-start targets rather than only shifting the average.

The paired seed-level difference for global warm start versus independent BO is $-3.567$ experiments, with 95\% bootstrap CI $(-4.908,-2.333)$ and Wilcoxon $p=0.0005$. Fifteen of 20 confirmatory seeds favor global warm start. The result is also consistent across all three vehicle families: family-level mean $\Nfive$ lies between 6.4 and 6.9 for independent BO and between 3.0 and 3.1 for both fleet-informed methods.

\subsection{Dynamics-aware weighting adds no measurable benefit}

The second pre-registered hypothesis asks whether the identified dynamics should determine how strongly historical observations are trusted. On the confirmatory data, dynamics-weighted warm start is statistically indistinguishable from global pooling. The paired seed-level mean difference in $\Nfive$ is $-0.008$ experiments with 95\% CI $(-0.058,+0.033)$ and Wilcoxon $p=0.706$. Only two of 20 seeds favor dynamics weighting. The secondary early-window $\AURCstar$ gives the same conclusion: global and dynamics-weighted warm start differ by only $-0.0005$, with CI $(-0.0050,+0.0041)$ and $p=0.936$.

This null result is not caused by an ineffective weighting implementation. The dynamics weighting strongly attenuates some cross-family observations and can change the first BO proposal. Rather, the landscape study provides a structural explanation: the calibration objective is far more invariant than the underlying plant. All evaluated client pairs have landscape rank correlation $\rho_{ij}^{J}\ge0.95$, including dynamically distant pairs. Consequently, an observation collected on another vehicle family still contains useful information about which regions of the common $\xi$-space are promising.

A separate development-stage coverage study further tested this explanation by limiting the mature prior to nine peers and varying the number of same-family sources from five down to zero. Dynamics weighting did not outperform global pooling at any coverage level, including the zero-same-family case. Restricting the source pool to only same-family peers was instead slightly worse than retaining all available observations, indicating that, in this low-dimensional calibration problem, data quantity is more valuable than family-based source selection.

\subsection{Secondary regret analysis}

The early-window $\AURCstar$ confirms the primary count-based result with a continuous metric. Global warm start improves the seed-level mean $\AURCstar$ by $-0.3469$ relative to independent BO, with 95\% CI $(-0.4514,-0.2496)$ and $p<0.0001$; all 20 confirmatory seeds favor global warm start. Dynamics-weighted warm start yields an almost identical improvement of $-0.3474$, with 19 of 20 seeds favorable. The consistency between threshold-based and continuous metrics rules out the possibility that the main result is an artifact of the discrete 5\% threshold.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The main practical conclusion is that a mature fleet should be treated as a reusable source of controller-calibration information. In the studied LQI problem, a newly arriving target reaches near-oracle performance after roughly three local experiments when historical fleet observations are available, compared with more than six experiments under independent BO. The effect is especially pronounced in the tail: almost every warm-started target reaches the 5\% criterion within five evaluations, while fewer than half of independently optimized targets do so.

The more surprising result concerns heterogeneity. The vehicle families differ in mass, yaw inertia, and tire properties, and the identified dynamics are clearly distinguishable. Nevertheless, those differences do not induce equally different rankings over the LQI weight space. This distinction is important for learning-enabled control: \emph{plant heterogeneity need not imply optimization-task heterogeneity}. A representation that is useful for grouping dynamical models can therefore be unnecessary for transferring higher-level controller-calibration experience.

This observation also explains why simple pooling outperforms more selective source use in some development conditions. In a three-dimensional search space with very little target data, additional historical observations improve GP coverage even when they come from dynamically different clients. Soft dynamics weighting does not harm performance, but it does not provide a measurable advantage either. The result should not be interpreted as a general statement that dynamics-aware transfer is never useful. It states that, for the present fleet and LQI calibration task, the calibration landscapes are sufficiently universal that explicit source selection is unnecessary.

Several limitations remain. First, validation is simulation based; the true plant is nonlinear and intentionally mismatched to the controller-design model, but real-vehicle confirmation is still missing. Second, the study considers one controller structure, one three-dimensional calibration parameterization, and one lateral tracking application. Higher-dimensional or more task-specific calibration problems may exhibit less transferable landscapes. Third, the present architecture assumes that scalar historical calibration records can be centrally stored or otherwise made available to the target; it does not claim privacy of these records. Raw identification and rollout trajectories need not be shared, but formal federated or privacy-preserving implementations are outside the present scope. Finally, the mature histories themselves require prior fleet experimentation. Their cost is amortized across future targets rather than eliminated.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This paper investigated whether controller-calibration experience accumulated by a mature heterogeneous fleet can reduce the commissioning effort for a newly arriving client. In a pre-registered held-out study on nonlinear vehicle lateral dynamics, warm-starting Bayesian optimization with historical fleet observations reduced the mean number of target-local experiments required to reach within 5\% of the client-specific oracle from 6.63 to 3.07, while substantially reducing variability and difficult cold-start cases. Dynamics-aware source weighting did not improve over simple global pooling. A separate calibration-landscape analysis explains this result: despite substantial dynamics heterogeneity, the ranking of LQI weight configurations remains highly consistent across clients. The results therefore indicate that mature fleet experience can be broadly reusable for data-efficient controller calibration, and that the heterogeneity relevant for plant modeling need not be the heterogeneity that matters for controller-parameter optimization.
