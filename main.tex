\title{Fleet-Informed Bayesian Optimization for Data-Efficient Controller Calibration}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Jakob Weber, Markus Gurtner, Adrian Trachte, Andreas Kugi}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Draft author list -- affiliations to be finalized}}}

\begin{abstract}
\color{blue}
Controller calibration is often repeated for every new system variant, although previously calibrated fleet members already contain information about promising controller parameters. This paper investigates whether such fleet knowledge can reduce the number of new closed-loop experiments required to calibrate another client. We consider linear-quadratic integral (LQI) yaw-rate control for a heterogeneous vehicle fleet and use Bayesian optimization (BO) to tune three controller-weight parameters. Historical scalar calibration data from previously calibrated clients initialize the Gaussian-process surrogate of a new client. Each proposed parameter vector is converted into an LQI gain using the new client's own identified model and is then evaluated on its nonlinear plant. Across 120 new-client calibration problems from 20 held-out fleet realizations, fleet-informed BO reduces the mean number of new experiments required to reach within 5\% of a client-specific offline reference from 6.63 to 3.07, a reduction of 54\%. Dynamics-aware weighting of the historical data provides no measurable improvement over unweighted pooling. A separate landscape study shows why. Although the plant dynamics differ substantially, the LQI calibration landscapes remain highly rank-correlated, with Spearman correlation above 0.95 for every evaluated client pair. The historical data require experiments on earlier fleet members, but this cost is incurred once and can be reused when later clients are commissioned.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Bayesian optimization, controller calibration, transfer learning, LQI, heterogeneous systems, vehicle control
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\color{blue}
Controller calibration is an important but expensive part of commissioning modern dynamical systems. Even when the controller structure follows from model-based design, parameters such as linear-quadratic regulator or integral-control weights determine the final compromise between tracking performance, control effort, and robustness. In practice, these parameters are often refined through repeated simulation or closed-loop testing. Bayesian optimization (BO) is attractive for this task because it can optimize low-dimensional controller parameters from a limited number of expensive evaluations~\cite{frazier2018tutorial,marco2016lqr,berkenkamp2016safe,schillinger2017safe,vonrohr2024local}.

A fleet setting changes the calibration problem fundamentally. Vehicles or machines are commissioned one after another, so previously calibrated clients already contain closed-loop evaluations collected during earlier commissioning. Repeating a cold-start optimization for every new client discards this accumulated information. Reusing historical records does not eliminate the cost of generating them. Instead, that cost is incurred on earlier clients and can be amortized across later fleet members, while the new client requires only the experiments that must be executed on its own plant.

Transfer-learning and collaborative BO methods address related cold-start and multi-agent optimization problems by reusing information from related tasks or agents~\cite{joy2019transfer,tighineanu2022transfer,perrone2018scalable,dai2020fts,dai2021dpfts,chen2025collaborative}. For controller calibration, however, the key question is how transferable previous calibration observations remain when the underlying dynamical systems differ.

The answer is not obvious. Heterogeneous vehicle dynamics can change closed-loop behavior substantially, and uncertainty-aware dynamics estimates contain useful structure for clustered system identification and controller design~\cite{weber2026ifac}. This motivates dynamics-aware transfer, where observations from dynamically similar clients are trusted more strongly. At the same time, the controller-calibration problem may be more invariant than the plant itself. Different plant models can induce similar rankings of the same low-dimensional LQI weight configurations. In that case, broad reuse of fleet experience may be sufficient and explicit source selection may add little.

This paper studies that distinction for an existing fleet and a newly arriving client. Previously calibrated clients, which we call \emph{source clients}, provide scalar records containing an evaluated controller-weight vector and its normalized closed-loop cost. The new client begins with two local initialization experiments. It then fits a Gaussian-process (GP) surrogate using its own measurements together with the source records and sequentially evaluates new LQI calibrations. The historical source records were collected before the new client is commissioned. They therefore require no new source-client experiments during this process. Only the new client's own closed-loop evaluations are counted when we report commissioning effort. We transfer calibration experience in the common weight space, not feedback gains. For every proposed weight vector, the new client computes its own LQI gain from its identified model before evaluating the controller.

The final evaluation protocol, including the compared methods, local experiment budget, primary metric, and statistical tests, was fixed before the 20 final fleet realizations were examined. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate \emph{fleet-informed controller calibration} as a transfer problem in which historical calibration observations from previously commissioned clients are reused to reduce the number of new closed-loop experiments required by another client.
    \item In a held-out evaluation on a heterogeneous nonlinear vehicle fleet, fleet-informed BO reduces the mean number of local evaluations needed to reach within 5\% of a client-specific offline reference from 6.63 to 3.07 and strongly reduces the difficult-client tail.
    \item We characterize why broad transfer is possible. Although the identified vehicle dynamics differ substantially, the LQI calibration landscapes remain highly rank-correlated. Consistent with this observation, dynamics-aware weighting does not outperform simple unweighted pooling on the held-out fleets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\color{blue}

\subsection{Heterogeneous clients and local controller design}

Consider clients $i\in\mathcal C$ with true nonlinear plants
\begin{align}
 \mathbf{x}_{i,k+1}&=\mathbf{f}_i(\mathbf{x}_{i,k},u_{i,k},\mathbf{w}_{i,k}),\\
 \mathbf{y}_{i,k}&=\mathbf{h}_i(\mathbf{x}_{i,k},\mathbf{v}_{i,k}),
 \label{eq:true_plant_generic}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{f}_i$ differs across clients. Each client identifies a compact linear control-design model
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{x}_{k+1}
 =
 \mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_i)\mathbf{x}_k
 +
 \mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_i)u_k.
 \label{eq:identified_model}
\end{equation}
Local recursive least-squares identification yields the parameter estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i$ and the associated parameter covariance $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_i$. In the vehicle study, the state is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{x}=[\beta,r]^\top,
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is vehicle sideslip and $r$ is yaw rate. The true evaluation plant includes nonlinear tire saturation, while identification and LQI synthesis use the lower-order linear model.

For yaw-rate tracking, the state is augmented with the integral tracking error
\begin{equation}
 z_{k+1}=z_k+T_s\left(r_k^{\mathrm{ref}}-r_k\right).
 \label{eq:integral_state}
\end{equation}
With
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{x}_k^a=[\mathbf{x}_k^\top,z_k]^\top,
\end{equation}
the LQI penalty is parameterized by the three-dimensional vector
$\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi\subset\mathbb R^3$. The augmented-state channels have different units and characteristic magnitudes. We therefore use the fixed scale vector
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{s}
 =
 [0.053229,\,0.575573,\,0.139862]^\top
 \label{eq:state_scales}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{align}
 \mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
 &=
 \operatorname{diag}\left(
 \frac{10^{\xi_1}}{s_1^2},
 \frac{10^{\xi_2}}{s_2^2},
 \frac{10^{\xi_3}}{s_3^2}
 \right),\\
 \mathbf{R}&=1.
 \label{eq:lqi_weights}
\end{align}
The scaling places the three state channels on comparable physical ranges. Consequently, the components of $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ primarily control relative penalty strength instead of compensating for different units.

For client $i$, each candidate $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is converted into a client-specific gain
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{K}_i(\boldsymbol{\xi})
 =
 \operatorname{LQI}\!\left(
 \hat{\mathbf{A}}_i,
 \hat{\mathbf{B}}_i,
 \hat{\mathbf{C}}_i,
 \mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),
 \mathbf{R}
 \right).
 \label{eq:lqi_gain}
\end{equation}
The term \emph{client-specific} means that the same calibration vector $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ can produce a different feedback gain for every client because the gain is recomputed from that client's identified model. The transferable quantity is therefore the location and observed performance of a calibration point in the common $\boldsymbol{\xi}$-space.

For calibration episode $\omega$, applying $\mathbf{K}_i(\boldsymbol{\xi})$ to the true plant yields the yaw-rate tracking cost $J_i(\boldsymbol{\xi},\omega)$. The client objective is
\begin{equation}
 f_i(\boldsymbol{\xi})
 =
 \mathbb E_{\omega}\!\left[J_i(\boldsymbol{\xi},\omega)\right].
 \label{eq:client_objective}
\end{equation}
Only feasible controller candidates are considered valid calibration outcomes. The feasibility conditions are defined in Sec.~\ref{subsec:vehicle_task} and include successful controller synthesis together with limits on sideslip and steering rate.

\subsection{Fleet-informed calibration objective}

A previously calibrated source client $j$ provides records
\begin{equation}
 \calD_j^{\mathrm{hist}}
 =
 \left\{
 \left(
 \boldsymbol{\xi}_{j,m},
 \tilde y_{j,m}
 \right)
 \right\}_{m=1}^{M_j},
 \label{eq:historical_data}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 \tilde y_{j,m}
 =
 \frac{J_j(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j,m})}
 {J_j^{\mathrm{base}}}
 \label{eq:historical_normalization}
\end{equation}
is the cost normalized by the fixed baseline controller of source client $j$. This normalization is introduced here so that historical observations from clients with different absolute tracking-error scales can be combined.

For a newly arriving client $i$, all historical records belonging to that same client are excluded from the source pool. This held-out construction emulates a genuinely new client that has not previously completed the calibration procedure. It also prevents leakage from a fully optimized history of the plant that is currently being calibrated.

The practical quantity of interest is the number of new experiments performed on client $i$. Let $J_i^{\mathrm{ref}}$ denote a client-specific offline reference cost obtained from a separate, dense evaluation procedure and used only for reporting. We define
\begin{equation}
 N_{i,5\%}
 =
 \min\left\{
 n:
 J_i^{\mathrm{best}}(n)
 \le
 1.05J_i^{\mathrm{ref}}
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:n5}
\end{equation}
The central question is whether access to historical fleet data reduces $N_{i,5\%}$ compared with independent BO.

\color{blue}

\subsection{Normalized historical observations}

The normalization in~\eqref{eq:historical_normalization} is also applied to every observation collected by the new client. The baseline controller is fixed before BO begins and is independent of the offline reference used for reporting. Infeasible evaluations receive the same fixed penalty in normalized units. This creates a common response scale for fitting one surrogate to data from multiple clients.

\subsection{Warm-started GP surrogate}

At local evaluation $n$, client $i$ fits a GP surrogate to
\begin{equation}
 \calD_i^{(n)}
 =
 \calD_i^{\mathrm{local}}(n)
 \cup
 \bigcup_{j\ne i}\calD_j^{\mathrm{hist}}.
 \label{eq:pooled_dataset}
\end{equation}
After the local initialization, the next calibration vector is selected with expected improvement~\cite{frazier2018tutorial},
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol{\xi}_{i,n+1}
 \in
 \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi}
 \operatorname{EI}_i
 \left(
 \boldsymbol{\xi}
 \mid
 \calD_i^{(n)}
 \right).
 \label{eq:ei}
\end{equation}
The new client then synthesizes $\mathbf{K}_i(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i,n+1})$ and evaluates the resulting controller on its own nonlinear plant. The source records have already been collected and remain fixed throughout this commissioning procedure. Using them therefore requires no additional rollouts on the source clients.

We compare three methods. Independent BO uses only observations collected on the new client. Global fleet warm start combines all available source records with equal weight. Dynamics-weighted warm start uses the same records but attenuates observations from dynamically dissimilar source clients. The latter is included as a mechanistic ablation.

\subsection{Dynamics-weighted ablation}

Because local identification provides both $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_i$, we can quantify pairwise dynamics similarity. For clients $i$ and $j$, define
\begin{equation}
(\distdyn_{ij})^2
=
\left(
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_j
\right)^\top
\left(
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_i
+
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_j
+
\epsilon\mathbf{I}
\right)^{-1}
\left(
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_j
\right)
\label{eq:dyn_distance}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 s_{ij}
 =
 \exp\left(
 -\frac{(\distdyn_{ij})^2}{2\ell_d^2}
 \right),
 \label{eq:dyn_similarity}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_d$ is the median pairwise-distance bandwidth. Lower source similarity is converted into larger GP observation noise,
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{ij}
 =
 \frac{\alpha_0}
 {\max(s_{ij},\epsilon_s)}.
 \label{eq:weighted_noise}
\end{equation}
The weighting changes the fitted surrogate and can change the selected acquisition point. Its usefulness is therefore tested empirically rather than assumed.

\section{Experimental Design}
\color{blue}

\subsection{Vehicle model, fleet, and tracking task}
\label{subsec:vehicle_task}

The study uses a fixed-speed lateral vehicle benchmark derived from the standard small-angle bicycle model~\cite{weber2026ifac}. The control-design model has state $\mathbf{x}=[\beta,r]^\top$, steering input $\delta$, and constant longitudinal speed $U$. Its continuous-time dynamics are
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\mathbf{x}}
 =
 \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
 +
 \mathbf{B}\delta,
 \label{eq:bicycle_ct}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-\dfrac{C_f+C_r}{mU}
&
\dfrac{-aC_f+bC_r}{mU^2}-1
\\[2mm]
\dfrac{-aC_f+bC_r}{I_z}
&
-\dfrac{a^2C_f+b^2C_r}{I_zU}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:bicycle_A}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{C_f}{mU}
\\[2mm]
\dfrac{aC_f}{I_z}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:bicycle_B}
\end{equation}
The model is discretized by zero-order hold with sampling time $T_s=0.05$ s.

Controller candidates are evaluated on a nonlinear version of the same lateral plant. The front and rear lateral tire forces use the smooth saturation law
\begin{equation}
 F_y
 =
 \mu F_z
 \tanh\left(
 \frac{C_\alpha\alpha}
 {\mu F_z}
 \right),
 \label{eq:tire_nonlinearity}
\end{equation}
with $\mu=1.0$. Thus, the plant used for performance evaluation is intentionally more nonlinear than the model used for LQI synthesis.

The fleet contains 30 clients in every realization, with ten clients from each of three families. Table~\ref{tab:vehicle_params} lists the family prototypes. Payload clients increase mass by 20\% and yaw inertia by 25\%. Tire-degraded clients reduce front cornering stiffness by 25\%. The generator additionally introduces small client-to-client variation around each family prototype using a variability setting of 0.025. Family labels are used only to construct and analyze the benchmark. They are not provided to BO.

Each BO evaluation measures yaw-rate root-mean-square error over a common pair of lane-change maneuvers. Their lane-change half-durations are $T_{\mathrm{lc}}=1.0$ s and $T_{\mathrm{lc}}=2.0$ s. Every controller candidate is evaluated on three stochastic episodes of each maneuver, giving six calibration episodes per BO query. All clients use this same balanced task distribution so that differences in the calibration objective arise from the plant rather than from different assigned maneuvers.

Yaw-rate RMSE is the calibration metric. A candidate is marked infeasible if LQI synthesis fails or if the nonlinear closed-loop rollout violates the prescribed limits. The sideslip magnitude is limited to $6^\circ$. The steering-rate change is limited to $0.2618$ rad per simulation step. These feasibility conditions are applied consistently to the baseline controller, source histories, and new-client evaluations.

\subsection{Landscape-similarity study}

To test whether dynamics heterogeneity translates into calibration heterogeneity, selected clients are evaluated on the same 32-point Sobol design
\begin{equation}
 \left\{
 \boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}
 \right\}_{m=1}^{32}.
\end{equation}
A Sobol design is a deterministic low-discrepancy sequence that provides space-filling coverage of the three-dimensional calibration box. For clients $i$ and $j$, pairwise landscape similarity is measured by
\begin{equation}
 \rho_{ij}^{J}
 =
 \operatorname{Spearman}\left(
 \{J_i(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^{32},
 \{J_j(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^{32}
 \right).
 \label{eq:landscape_similarity}
\end{equation}
The key comparison is $\rho_{ij}^{J}$ versus $\distdyn_{ij}$.

\subsection{Held-out fleet-informed BO evaluation}

The final experiment uses 20 fleet realizations that were not used for method development. Each realization contains 30 previously calibrated source clients, with ten clients from each family. Every source client contributes an independent BO history of 25 evaluations. Six clients per realization, two from each family, are then treated in turn as newly arriving clients. For each such client, its own completed history is removed and the remaining $29\times25=725$ source observations are available before its calibration begins. This produces 120 held-out new-client calibration problems.

Each new client starts with $n_{\rm init}=2$ local points from a Sobol low-discrepancy sequence. These points provide space-filling initialization of the three-dimensional search box before expected improvement starts selecting candidates. The client then receives at most ten BO-selected evaluations, giving a total local budget of 12 experiments. Independent BO, global warm start, and dynamics-weighted warm start use the same local initialization and the same evaluation randomness.

The primary metric is $\Nfive$ from~\eqref{eq:n5}. As a secondary continuous metric, we use reference-relative regret
\begin{equation}
 r_i^{\mathrm{ref}}(n)
 =
 \frac{
 J_i^{\mathrm{best}}(n)-J_i^{\mathrm{ref}}
 }{
 J_i^{\mathrm{ref}}
 }.
 \label{eq:regretref}
\end{equation}
We also report the area under this regret curve over the early local budget and the probabilities of reaching the 5\% criterion within three and five local experiments. Fleet realization is the statistical replication unit. Metrics are first averaged over the six held-out new clients within each realization, then compared across the 20 realizations using paired bootstrap confidence intervals and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

\color{blue}

\subsection{Fleet warm start halves local experimentation}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} shows reference-relative regret versus the number of new experiments performed on each held-out client. Both fleet-informed methods improve markedly faster than independent BO. Global and dynamics-weighted warm start remain nearly indistinguishable.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\figurepdf{0.82\textwidth}{figures/confirmatory_convergence.pdf}
\caption{\textcolor{blue}{Held-out convergence over 20 fleet realizations and 120 new-client calibration problems. Fleet warm start accelerates controller calibration from the first BO iterations. Dynamics weighting and unweighted pooling exhibit essentially identical convergence.}}
\label{fig:convergence}

Independent BO requires on average 6.63 local experiments to reach within 5\% of the client-specific offline reference, compared with 3.07 for global fleet warm start. This is a 54\% reduction in mean new-client experimentation. The standard deviation decreases from 3.76 to 0.81, which shows that warm start also removes most of the long tail of difficult cold-start clients. The paired realization-level global-minus-independent difference is $-3.567$ experiments, with 95\% bootstrap CI $(-4.908,-2.333)$ and Wilcoxon $p=0.0005$. The result is consistent across all three vehicle families.

\subsection{Dynamics-aware weighting adds no measurable benefit}

On the held-out fleets, dynamics-weighted warm start is statistically indistinguishable from global pooling. The paired realization-level mean difference in $\Nfive$ is $-0.008$ experiments with 95\% CI $(-0.058,+0.033)$ and Wilcoxon $p=0.706$. The secondary early-budget regret analysis gives the same conclusion, with a difference of $-0.0005$, CI $(-0.0050,+0.0041)$, and $p=0.936$.

The null result is structural rather than numerical. The dynamics weighting strongly attenuates some cross-family observations and can change acquisition decisions. However, the calibration objective is much more invariant than the plant dynamics. All evaluated client pairs have $\rho_{ij}^{J}\ge0.95$. A separate coverage experiment also restricted the available history to nine source clients and varied the number of same-family sources from five down to zero. Dynamics weighting did not outperform global pooling at any coverage level. Restricting the source set to same-family clients was slightly worse than retaining all available observations. Figure~\ref{fig:null_weighting} summarizes these findings.

\subsection{Continuous early-budget performance}

The primary metric $\Nfive$ is intentionally easy to interpret, but it depends on the selected 5\% threshold. We therefore use the area under the reference-relative regret curve as a secondary continuous measure of calibration progress. This metric integrates the quality of the best controller found over the early experiment budget and does not depend on a single threshold-crossing event.

Global warm start improves the realization-level mean early-budget $\AURCref$ by $-0.3469$ relative to independent BO, with 95\% CI $(-0.4514,-0.2496)$ and $p<0.0001$. All 20 held-out fleet realizations favor global warm start on this metric. Dynamics-weighted warm start yields an almost identical improvement of $-0.3474$. The agreement between $\Nfive$ and the continuous regret metric shows that the main sample-efficiency result is not an artifact of the 5\% criterion.

\color{black}
\section{Discussion}
\color{blue}

The main practical conclusion is that an existing fleet should be treated as a reusable source of controller-calibration information. In the studied LQI problem, a newly arriving client reaches near-reference performance after roughly three local experiments when historical fleet observations are available, compared with more than six experiments under independent BO. The benefit is especially pronounced in the tail. Almost every warm-started client reaches the 5\% criterion within five evaluations, while fewer than half of the independently optimized clients do so.

The more surprising result concerns heterogeneity. The vehicle families differ in mass, yaw inertia, and tire properties, and Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape} shows that their identified dynamics are clearly distinguishable. Nevertheless, these differences do not produce equally different rankings over the LQI weight space. Thus, \emph{plant heterogeneity need not imply optimization-task heterogeneity}. A representation that is useful for grouping dynamical models can therefore be unnecessary for transferring higher-level calibration experience.

This observation also explains why simple pooling can outperform more selective source use. In a three-dimensional search space with little new-client data, additional historical observations improve GP coverage even when they originate from dynamically different clients. Soft dynamics weighting does not materially harm performance, but it provides no measurable advantage in the present benchmark. This should not be interpreted as a general rejection of dynamics-aware transfer. It shows that, for the investigated vehicle families and LQI calibration objective, the calibration landscapes are sufficiently consistent that explicit source selection is unnecessary.

The historical source records are not free. Each was generated by experiments performed when earlier clients were commissioned. The economic argument is instead one of reuse. Once these evaluations have been collected, their cost can be distributed across subsequent clients. The 54\% reduction reported here therefore quantifies the additional experimentation required for a new client after a fleet has already accumulated calibration experience.

Several limitations remain. Validation is simulation based. The nonlinear evaluation plant is intentionally mismatched to the controller-design model, but real-vehicle confirmation is still missing. The study considers one controller structure, one three-dimensional calibration parameterization, and one lateral tracking application. Higher-dimensional or more task-specific calibration problems may exhibit less transferable landscapes. The present architecture assumes that scalar historical calibration records can be stored centrally or otherwise made available to the new client and does not provide a formal privacy guarantee.

\color{black}
\section{Conclusion}
\color{blue}

This paper investigated whether controller-calibration experience accumulated by previously commissioned fleet members can reduce the commissioning effort for a newly arriving client. In a held-out study on nonlinear vehicle lateral dynamics, warm-starting Bayesian optimization with historical fleet observations reduced the mean number of new-client experiments required to reach within 5\% of a client-specific offline reference from 6.63 to 3.07 while substantially reducing variability. Dynamics-aware source weighting did not improve over simple global pooling. A calibration-landscape analysis explains this result. Despite substantial dynamics heterogeneity, the ranking of LQI weight configurations remains highly consistent across clients. Historical fleet experience can therefore be reused for data-efficient controller calibration, and the heterogeneity that matters for plant modeling need not be the heterogeneity that matters for controller-parameter optimization.
