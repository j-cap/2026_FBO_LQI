Why do the LQI weights transfer so well?

Andreas’s question: if vehicle calibration landscapes are similar, why optimize again on the target instead of reusing good weights?

Current answer: in this pilot, source-calibrated weights meet the 5% performance criterion on every tested target. The target still adapts through model-based gain computation. Target weight optimization is not needed to meet this criterion.

1 · We transfer preferences, not a fixed controller

Source weights ξ → target model + target actuator τ → new LQI gains K and reference gain

The same weights express the same design preferences, while the identified target dynamics determine the feedback law. The simulation retains the same tracking task, controller structure and normalization across vehicles.

2 · Direct transfer passes without selecting a favorable donor

Six targets, five other donor vehicles per target, three source-search repeats and five actuator time constants (0.10–0.40 s). Weights were selected only on each donor’s calibration data. Gains were recomputed on the target; no target weight search or best-donor selection occurred.

3 · Gain redesign matters; measurement noise is not the whole explanation

For a retrospectively identified common weight vector, we held weights fixed and compared target redesign with gains frozen at τ = 0.20 s. We also froze gains from the same-family vehicle in the other fleet realization. True and measured errors were logged on identical noisy rollouts.

Freezing the same vehicle’s gains increases median measured cost by 5.0% at τ = 0.30 s and 13.2% at 0.40 s, relative to redesign. At 0.20 s the two controllers are identical. At 0.10 s no frozen-gain episode passes feasibility at both resolutions, predominantly because of the actuator-rate limit. At 0.15 s all frozen-gain banks fail numerical convergence checks; their cost estimates are excluded.

Measurement noise contributes about 50% of measured MSE for the redesigned common-weight controller. Nevertheless, its worst gap to the frozen reference is only 0.10% in measured RMSE and 0.13% in true-state RMSE. Shared weights work well even when scoring true error. This check concerns the common vector, not all 1,800 source-only transfers.

4 · The good-weight regions overlap strongly

On matched 256-point calibration grids, family-pair rank correlations are at least 0.988. Each grid has 38–79 points within 5% of its own best cost; median overlap of these sets is 0.864 (Jaccard). Thus modest source searches can land in a region that is also good for another vehicle. The outer objective rewards yaw tracking, with sideslip and actuator quantities as hard constraints; it does not continuously penalize effort or comfort.

Interpretation: similar tuning preferences, overlapping acceptable weight regions and model-based gain redesign together explain the observed transfer success. These results support direct weight reuse in this setup—not a demonstrated need for fleet-informed target BO.
Scope and qualifications. Six familiar vehicles from two fleet realizations; simulation only. The actuator time constant is known. State feedback uses noise-free simulated states; yaw noise enters the integrator and score. The common-vector diagnostic is retrospective, and the reference was selected for measured error, not reoptimized for true error. Finite-horizon feasibility is not a stability proof. Fixed-gain results at τ = 0.15 s remain numerically unresolved. Earlier anti-windup sign errors were corrected; original ideal-steering results still need their own impact audit.
